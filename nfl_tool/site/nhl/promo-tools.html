King of the Crease

DraftKings pays a share of $500K in bonus bets to everyone whose goalie makes the most saves of the day (ties split it; if your goalie doesn't start, the team's starter counts for you). Saves come from shots, so this ranks every team's starter by how many shots tonight's matchup points to. Upside is his chance to finish with the day's most saves. Value is that chance against what his betting line alone says: above 1.00 the market may be underselling him, and a goalie fewer people are on pays more if he wins. The qualifying bet has to be +100 or longer, so when a line's over is priced below that, bet the next line up.

How the numbers are made, and how far to trust them

Projected saves = a league-average start × how much the other team shoots × how many shots his own team gives up × home or away and rest × his own save rate, all from this season's games. Each team's number is its games so far mixed with ten games of a league-average team, so one wild night can't run away with it.

What a replay of the last two seasons showed (5,034 starts, each projected only from that season's games before it):

  • The two teams are nearly all of it. The other team's shot volume moves a projection about 6% either way, and so does what his own team allows. The road goalie sees about 3% more than the home one; a team on the second night of a back-to-back gives up about 3% more and shoots about 3% less.
  • The goalie himself barely matters: his save rate is worth about 1%. His own saves a start so far predicted worse than calling every goalie average. Scoring chances from in close, team strength and recent form added nothing.
  • The chances were honest. Goalies given 10 to 14% to lead the day led it 10% of the time; the model's favorite led 18% of days (a random pick: 8%), and its top three had the day's leader on 42%. The day's best averaged 37 saves.
  • Even the best pick loses most days. That is the promo, not the model: about 1 start in 20 ends early, overtime adds shots at random, and twenty goalies are in the draw.
  • Early in a season it knows little: in the first two weeks its projection was only a hair better than calling every goalie average, and it was at full strength by about game 20.

The line counts too. A book's saves line knows things a few weeks of games can't: who is hurt, what a team was before this season. So a goalie's chance to lead the day is worked out from his line, moved toward the model by as much as the season so far has earned: a few percent of the way in October, halfway from about game 20 on. A goalie with no line posted yet is on the model alone. Proj is always the model's own number, so you can see where it and the line disagree.

Value compares two chances for the same goalie against the same field: his chance to lead the day, and what that chance would be if he were exactly what his line says. Only his own number changes, so it can't come from the rest of the slate. It gets the tag at 1.15 or more when he has a real chance to begin with. When the model and the line are 4 or more saves apart the box turns amber: a gap that big is more often news the model can't see than a gift. Expect few Value tags until teams have played 10 to 15 games.

What it has not shown: that it beats the line, or how many people will be on each goalie before puck drop. There is no free history of saves lines to test against, and DraftKings shows only a few payouts after each contest. So the page keeps its own record of every finished day: the Winners list shows who led each one, where the page had him, and what DraftKings paid.

Starters are never confirmed here. A starter is official at warmups, and nothing the site can read carries that. It has two outside sources: the first goalie in the NHL's projected lineup for the game (posted around midday Eastern; last season he started 92% of the time, and only about half the time when it named the goalie who had started the night before), and the goalie the book has a saves line for. A check mark means the two agree. A warning with a second name means they disagree: the book's goalie is shown and the other is named beside him. No mark means only one source has spoken, or neither (then it is the team's usual starter, or the other goalie on the second night of a back-to-back). The promo counts whoever starts for the team, so the numbers in a row barely move with the name; the name is what to check before you bet. Lines are Novig's; DraftKings' own line can sit half a save either side.

Icebreaker

DraftKings splits a prize pool among everyone with a qualifying first goal scorer bet on the player who scores the quickest goal of the 7:00 PM ET slate, by game clock (a goal 0:10 into a 7:08 puck drop beats one 0:15 into a 7:02 one). Chance is each player's chance to be that player among the games picked: his expected goals tonight (our Goals model, moved toward his anytime price) over everyone's. A team's chance is its players' added up; click a team to see only its players. The Fast starts numbers are this season's, shown for context and not counted: on two past seasons they predicted nothing that a team's normal scoring didn't (see Historical Data). Prices are DraftKings' first goal scorer odds; a grey price is another book's when DraftKings has none yet.

What predicted it, and what didn't

  • Who scores early is who scores. A player's scoring rate x his ice time picked the scorer of a team's early goal best. That is what the Chance column is built on.
  • Teams' fast starts didn't help. Goals in the first 10 minutes, goals in the 1st period and the time of the first goal, for and against, predicted early scoring no better than a team's normal goals for and against. With a handful of games each they are mostly noise, so they are shown but not counted.
  • Early shots don't matter. A player's shots in the first 5 minutes picked his team's early scorer no better than a blind guess.
  • The first shift is a coin flip. Only about 1 winner in 3 was on the ice for the opening faceoff, and coaches change who starts: a forward who started one game started the next only 40% of the time.
  • The first two minutes are slow. About 15-20% fewer goals than the rest of the 1st period, for every team alike, so it doesn't change who to pick.
  • Defensemen scored 10-15% of the winning goals, and about 1 in 5 came on a power play.
  • Picking the team first helps little. The top-ranked team produced the winner 14% of the time (a team picked at random: 10%), and the top three teams 40% (three at random: 30%).

How to use it

This promo is mostly luck. The best pick will lose 49 times out of 50. The list narrows 150-plus skaters down to the ones who carry the most of the slate's scoring; the rest is the bet and the price. GMG's advice: have fun.
