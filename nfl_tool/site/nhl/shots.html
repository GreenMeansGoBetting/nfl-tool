How the number is made, and how far to trust it

Shots per minute × minutes tonight × tonight's matchup, all from this season's games. His shot rate comes from every minute he has played (recent games count a little more), read mostly from his shot attempts, which settle down sooner than shots on goal. His minutes come from his last few games. The matchup is what the other team allows: shots on goal overall, to his position, from the parts of the ice he shoots from, and on the power play, plus home ice and back-to-backs.

Tonight's lines. The NHL posts a projected lineup for every game around midday Eastern: the forward lines, the defense pairs, who is scratched and who is hurt. The page reads it, lists exactly the skaters in it, and moves a player's projection when he is on a busier or quieter line than his own minutes say (a fourth-liner filling in on the top line for an injured teammate). In the replay a fill-in did shoot more, but less than his new line's ice time suggests: a 12-minute forward listed on the first line took about 10% more shots, a fourth-liner on the second line about 24% more. He doesn't inherit the star's minutes or power play. A play that goes against tonight's line change (an under on a player moved up) is set apart as new line, not ranked. "Lineup Confirmed" means that lineup is in; it is the NHL's own projection from the morning skate, not the official sheet handed in at warmups. Last season about 95% of the skaters it listed did play.

This season only. Nothing from last season is in the model: not a player's old shot rate, not a defense's old numbers. Early in a season that means small samples, and two things follow from it.

  • Proj can sit well off his Avg. The model only partly trusts a handful of games: it mixes in an average skater at his position with his ice time (and an average defense for the other team), all from this season. When that puts a play against his own average, the row says vs his avg. In a replay of 2025-26, players whose average after 5 to 9 games said "over" while the model said "under" went over 36% of the time at a 1.5 line and 33% at 2.5: hot starts cooled. The record keeps those plays apart so you can see whether that holds.
  • There are a lot of big gaps to the market until about game 20. A star who starts slowly looks like an under to a model that has only seen this season; the book knows who he is. Those are the plays set apart at the bottom, marked check.

What a full-season replay showed (2025-26: every game projected only from that season's games before it, 44,496 player-games):

  • His own rate and his ice time are most of it. The other team's defense moves a projection about 6% either way; position 2%; where he shoots from 1%; the power play 1 to 2%; home ice +3%; a back-to-back about 3.5%.
  • A hot or cold last five games predicted nothing beyond his rate and minutes.
  • Trusting a handful of games outright would be a mistake: without the small-sample guard, projections missed by 5% more at 5 to 9 games and 13% more in a player's first four.
  • The chances were honest: players the model gave 60 to 70% to clear a line cleared it 63 to 65% of the time.
  • What it still can't see is the power-play units and anything decided after the lineup is posted (a late scratch, a line shuffle at warmups).

What it has not shown: that it beats the betting line. There is no free history of shots-on-goal prices to test against, and the market sees lineup news the model doesn't. So the page keeps its own record, above: every play graded at the price that was locked before puck drop. Until that record says otherwise, treat an edge as a spot worth a look, and a very large one (marked check) as a reason to look for news first.
