How the number is made, and how far to trust it

A stack is 2, 3 or 4 "1+ point" legs on teammates who are on the ice together: a line, a defense pair, a line and its pair, or two of the first power-play unit. When one of them scores, his linemates usually get the assists, so the legs hit together far more often than separate bets would. Each leg is the Points page's chance.

Fair is the price the stack's chance is worth, with no cut. The chance comes from "shared goals": each goal gives a point to a scorer and up to two assisters, so two legs hit together whenever one goal gives both a point. How often that happens was measured on two seasons of games for each kind of pair (linemates, a defense pair, a forward and his line's pair, power play), with the pair's own shared points this season mixed in, plus the power-play minutes the legs have played together and their combined expected goals. Correlation is how much more often the legs hit together than if they had nothing to do with each other; Non-correlated is that price, which no book will pay: every book prices correlation.

Score (1-100) assumes the book prices the correlation the way our model does, on its own leg prices, so the edge left is in the legs: 50 = no edge against Novig's leg prices, 60 = about 10% better. A leg Novig hasn't priced counts as no edge ("2 of 3 legs priced"). Books price correlation differently from each other and from us: use Copy to paste the stack into Pikkit or a book's builder and compare its price with Fair.

What the replays showed (2024-25 to fit, 2025-26 to check): a whole line got all three points twice as often as the legs multiplied; the chances were honest within about a point (the surest pairs a touch high). Stacks are made only from the NHL's projected lineup: it named the line that actually played 74% of the time, a player's usual linemates 31%. What it has not shown: that any stack beats the books. There is no free history of same-game parlay prices; the page keeps its own record.
